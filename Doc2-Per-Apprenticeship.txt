AoE2DE AI ENGINEERING KNOWLEDGE SYSTEM
DOCUMENT 2 — THE .PER ENGINEER'S APPRENTICESHIP
A zero-to-expert guide to reading, writing, debugging, designing, and engineering native AoE2DE AI

Companion to Document 1 — Forensic Engineering Reference

PURPOSE
-------
This is a book, not a command dictionary.

It is written for the person who opens an unfamiliar .per file and sees a wall of parentheses, names, numbers, jumps, timers, loads, and rules — and wants to understand what the author was trying to make the computer do.

The goal is not merely that the reader can write valid .per syntax. The goal is that, after working through this book, the reader can enter an unfamiliar AI, find its architecture, understand its state, follow information through several files, identify who owns important decisions, recognize the patterns experienced authors use, modify the system without breaking hidden contracts, and design new systems of their own.

The reader should eventually be able to look at a mature bot and think in two directions at once:

    What does this line literally do?

and:

    Why does a competent engineer need this mechanism here?

That second question is the dividing line between fluency and engineering.

STYLE AND PEDAGOGICAL INTENT
----------------------------
The explanatory character of this book is deliberately influenced by two traditions of technical writing: the concrete, patient, mechanism-first teaching associated with Charles Petzold, and the investigative, systems-oriented narrative curiosity associated with James Gleick. This is an influence on exposition, not an imitation of either author's prose.

The first influence contributes a habit of making invisible machinery visible: start with the thing the reader can see, take it apart carefully, name each moving piece, and show how the pieces cooperate. The second contributes a habit of treating difficult systems as things worth investigating: follow clues, notice strange behavior, compare competing explanations, and let the reader discover why an apparently peculiar mechanism exists.

The result should feel like an experienced engineer sitting beside the reader, opening a real AI, and saying: "Look here. This line matters. Now follow it. Who writes this value? Who reads it? What happens if it disappears? What happens if another module writes it?" The book should never hide technical depth in order to become approachable. It should make the depth approachable.

A note on evidence: this book distinguishes what the engine establishes, what the corpus demonstrates, what can be composed from multiple observations, and what is an engineering recommendation. The evidence vocabulary is inherited from Document 1:

CONFIRMED — directly established by authoritative engine or registry evidence.
DIRECT — directly observed in a real source or runtime artifact.
COMPOSED — supported by multiple independently observed facts whose relationship is explicit.
INFERRED — a reasoned interpretation not directly demonstrated.
AEGIS-GENERALIZATION — an engineering abstraction derived from observed systems.
UNCERTAIN — plausible but not adequately established.

A beautiful explanation is still not evidence. A compiler accepting a rule is still not proof that the rule produces the intended world state. A pattern appearing in Naga is not automatically an engine law. The reader is expected to learn both how to build systems and how to know what has actually been proved.

PART I — LEARNING TO SEE THE LANGUAGE
======================================================================

1. BEFORE THE FIRST RULE

There is a useful mistake to make when learning .per: assume, for a little while, that the code is telling you a story.

It is not a story in the ordinary programming-language sense. There is no comfortable sequence of functions marching from top to bottom. There is a collection of rules, state stores, commands, timers, loads, and control-flow tricks that together form a machine. The story is hidden in the relationships between them.

A mature AI therefore looks confusing for the same reason a city looks confusing from an airplane. You can see thousands of things, but you cannot yet see why the streets meet where they do.

Your first job is not to memorize every street.

Your first job is to learn what kind of thing a street is.

A .per engineer gradually develops this ability. A line such as:

    (defrule
        (can-train spearman-line)
     =>
        (train spearman-line))

starts as syntax. Then it becomes a production rule. Then it becomes an executor. Then, in a larger AI, the engineer asks the more important question: "Why is this executor here, and who decided that a spearman should be trained?"

That question opens the architecture.

A strong engineer learns to move continuously between scales:

    character → token → expression → rule → mechanism → state → subsystem → architecture → behavior

Beginners tend to stop at the first scale they understand. Experts keep moving.

This book is designed to teach that movement deliberately.


2. WHAT A .PER PROGRAM ACTUALLY IS

At first glance, an AI is just text. The engine reads the text, evaluates conditions, performs actions, and the game changes.

That description is true and almost useless.

The important mental model is this:

    WORLD
       ↓
    ENGINE OBSERVATION
       ↓
    .PER FACTS / STRATEGIC NUMBERS
       ↓
    RULE EVALUATION
       ↓
    GOALS / STRATEGIC NUMBERS / COMMANDS
       ↓
    ENGINE EFFECTS
       ↓
    WORLD

The loop closes.

A villager count is an observation. A goal storing a desired number of archers is software state. A train command is an engine request. A new archer appearing in the world is an effect. The next census observes that archer.

The same piece of information can therefore exist in several forms during its journey.

Suppose the enemy is producing cavalry.

One part of the AI may observe cavalry.
Another may classify the observation as a cavalry threat.
Another may decide that cavalry creates a requirement for anti-cavalry capability.
Another may raise a production dial.
Another may reserve resources.
Another may authorize a producer.
The producer may train a counter unit.
A verification layer may observe that the counter-unit count actually changed.

The beginner sees seven rules.

The engineer sees a control loop.

That distinction will recur throughout this book.


3. YOUR FIRST RULE: FACTS, ACTIONS, AND THE ARROW

The basic .per unit is a rule:

    (defrule
        CONDITIONS
     =>
        ACTIONS)

Read the arrow as a boundary between evidence and intervention.

The left side asks questions. The right side changes something.

A condition might ask whether a unit exists, whether a goal has a particular value, whether a timer is running, or whether an engine query returns a certain result.

An action might change a goal, train a unit, enable a timer, jump to another rule, or alter some other piece of AI state.

This sounds simple until you encounter a mature program.

Then the same command may appear in twenty files, the same goal may be written by five rule families, and a number that looks like a constant may actually be a goal value because of an operand prefix.

So learn a discipline immediately:

For every unfamiliar rule, ask five questions.

1. What must be true for this rule to fire?
2. What exactly does each action do?
3. Which state does each action read or write?
4. Who else writes that state?
5. What observable change should eventually appear in the game?

Do not skip question four because the rule "looks right."

Do not skip question five because the parser accepted it.

Those two omissions create an extraordinary number of AI bugs.


4. THE SEVEN PLACES INFORMATION CAN LIVE

A .per engineer must distinguish at least these categories:

1. constants
2. goals
3. strategic numbers
4. timers
5. engine/world facts
6. local rule-control state
7. actual world state

Constants are names for fixed values. They are useful because a meaningful name can replace an unexplained integer, but a constant is not a variable.

Goals are AI-side storage. They are programmable state and can represent counters, modes, targets, cursors, bookmarks, timestamps, and many other things.

Strategic numbers are engine-facing values. Some are true engine settings; others are reusable strategic-number slots whose interpretation depends on the AI.

Timers provide temporal state. They allow the AI to perform work periodically, create delays, and distinguish an event from an ongoing condition.

Facts expose engine observations or logical predicates.

Rule-control state includes things such as enabled/disabled rules and jump position. It is easy to overlook because it is embedded in the control structure rather than stored in an obvious variable.

World state is what actually happened: units trained, buildings completed, resources spent, units moving, battles occurring.

These are not interchangeable.

One of the most important habits in the entire profession is to stop saying "the value" and instead say "the goal," "the SN," "the timer state," "the constant," or "the world observation."

Precision in nouns produces precision in debugging.


5. CONSTANTS, GOALS, STRATEGIC NUMBERS, AND THE PREFIX PROBLEM

Native .per has an especially dangerous feature: similar-looking numbers can come from different stores.

Document 1 establishes the critical prefix distinction. When an operator expects a value, prefixes such as c:, g:, and s: select the relevant store:

    c:NAME   constant
    g:NAME   goal
    s:NAME   strategic number

This matters because the code can remain syntactically valid while reading the wrong cell.

A historical forensic example illustrates the danger. A rule using a goal operation with a strategic-number name can accidentally read an unrelated goal cell when the author intended an SN. A parser may accept the expression. The game may continue. The AI may then make decisions from a perfectly legal, perfectly wrong number.

Another classic error is treating a constant's identifier as though it were the value stored in a goal. For example, a form like:

    (set-goal bwc-regime bwc-best)

uses the constant value of bwc-best as the value written to the goal. If the desired behavior is to copy the current value of one goal into another, a goal-to-goal operation such as an appropriately formed up-modify-goal expression is required.

The lesson is larger than either example:

    A name is not a value.
    A value is not a store.
    A store is not world state.

When debugging, trace all four separately.


6. PASSES, SOURCE ORDER, AND WHY TIME EXISTS EVEN WITHOUT A CLOCK

A .per AI is evaluated repeatedly. Rules are not simply functions called once in the order a programmer might imagine.

Source order matters because rule evaluation and jump behavior depend on the compiled rule sequence. Actions such as disable-self affect subsequent passes. Jump distances are positional. Inserting a rule can therefore alter control flow even if the inserted rule seems unrelated to the logic around it.

This is why experienced engineers sometimes treat a section of .per as structurally frozen.

Imagine a jump that says, in effect, "skip the next twelve rules." The number twelve is not a semantic label. It is a physical distance in the compiled rule stream. Add two rules and the destination can change.

The language has no ordinary symbolic label mechanism that makes these regions immune to positional change.

This gives .per a peculiar engineering character: text editing can be a control-flow transformation.

The safest habit is to regard tuned jump regions as compiled artifacts. If you change them, re-audit every landing point.

This is also why preprocessor boundaries matter. Conditional compilation changes which rules exist. A jump that is safe in one configuration may land somewhere else in another.

The beginner sees a number.

The engineer sees an address encoded as a distance.


7. LOADING A BOT: THE MOMENT A FOLDER BECOMES A PROGRAM

A mature AI is usually not one enormous file because humans cannot work effectively that way. It is a root program that assembles modules.

The root is therefore more than a list of filenames. It is the architecture's entrance.

A canonical modular root tends to establish something like:

    constants
    initialization
    observation / threats
    economy
    construction
    production
    military
    strategy
    recovery / maintenance

The exact order varies. The principle does not: load order creates visibility and temporal assumptions.

Naga's root is a useful architectural specimen because its small entry file exposes a larger design behind it. Shadow provides the opposite specimen: a huge monolithic source where the architecture must be reconstructed from the organization of the rule stream rather than from a load graph.

This teaches an important lesson before the reader knows enough to appreciate it:

    modularity is a human organization technique, not automatic state isolation.

Two modules can be beautifully separated into files and still fight over the same goal.

A root file tells you where the rooms are.

It does not tell you who owns the keys.


8. THE FIRST DEBUGGING HABIT: NEVER GUESS WHAT A VALUE MEANS

When an AI behaves strangely, beginners often search for the rule that "looks suspicious."

Do something else first.

Find the state.

Suppose the bot suddenly trains too many skirmishers. Do not begin by changing the training rule. Ask:

    What value tells the producer how many skirmishers are wanted?
    Who writes it?
    Under what conditions?
    Is it a goal or SN?
    Does another rule lower it?
    Does escrow decrement it?
    Does expiry clear it?
    Does the producer read the same store?
    Is the producer actually the rule issuing the command?
    Did the world count change?

This is state-centered debugging.

It scales.

A suspicious rule is local. A state variable can lead you through the entire architecture.

PART II — LEARNING TO SEE PATTERNS

9. THE SHAPE OF AN EXPERIENCED AI

Once the syntax becomes familiar, something strange happens: large AIs begin to look repetitive.

You see the same ideas wearing different names.

A timer fires. A state is latched. A value is raised. A producer consumes it. A temporary condition expires. A guard prevents an action. A jump skips irrelevant work. A counter advances through a search. A policy chooses a target. An executor tests feasibility.

This repetition is not accidental.

Native .per is limited enough that experienced engineers repeatedly rediscover a relatively small set of useful control patterns.

Learning those patterns is much more powerful than memorizing isolated rules.

A pattern is a solution shape.

It answers a recurring problem:

    "I need this behavior, but the engine gives me these constraints. What shape reliably survives those constraints?"

The following chapters teach the major shapes as a beginner encounters them, then deepen them into engineering principles.


10. ONE-SHOT INITIALIZATION

Imagine the bot has a timer that must be enabled at startup.

You want:

    enable timer once
    then leave it alone

The natural pattern is a rule that fires at initialization and disables itself.

Conceptually:

    startup → establish state → disable initialization rule

The corpus contains hundreds of such rules in mature AIs. Stock contains large initialization blocks; Shadow contains substantial one-shot initialization as well.

Why does this pattern exist?

Because maintenance logic should not repeatedly overwrite the state it is maintaining.

The distinction is fundamental:

    initialization establishes a state
    maintenance preserves or changes a state

If initialization runs forever, maintenance cannot own the state cleanly.

The pattern also teaches the reader something subtle about .per: disable-self does not make a rule disappear from the positional rule stream. Jumps still count the rule. Therefore, one-shot rules inside positional jump regions remain structurally significant.

Engineering invariant:

    Anything downstream that requires initialized state must not execute before initialization establishes it.

Failure signature:

    values repeatedly return to defaults.

Or, if the initialization rule is accidentally omitted:

    maintenance assumes a state that was never established.


11. HEARTBEATS, TIMERS, AND THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN "ONCE" AND "AGAIN"

A timer turns an AI from a machine that reacts every pass into a machine that can choose its own rhythm.

A one-minute strategy review does not need to happen every evaluation pass. A five-second recovery check does not need to happen every minute.

Mature AIs therefore develop multiple time scales.

A heartbeat usually has three pieces:

    enable
    trigger
    re-arm

The third is the one beginners forget.

A rule that fires when a timer triggers is not necessarily periodic. If nobody re-arms the timer, the heartbeat has quietly become a one-shot.

Shadow demonstrates multiple periodic timer families, including one-minute, 30-second, two-minute, and three-minute patterns. Naga's initialization enables multiple timers, including its one-minute timer.

The engineering question is not merely "does timer 7 exist?"

It is:

    Who enables timer 7?
    Who consumes timer 7?
    Who re-arms timer 7?
    At what cadence?
    Can another subsystem re-arm it too?

The last question matters because shared timers create hidden coupling.

A timer is not just a clock.

It is a shared control resource.


12. REMEMBERING WHAT THE WORLD JUST TOLD YOU

The game world is volatile.

A threat can disappear from line of sight. A unit can move behind terrain. A census can return zero because the relevant observation is temporarily unavailable.

If the AI treats every zero as permanent truth, it becomes nervous and forgetful.

The solution is state latching.

The simplest conceptual lifecycle is:

    SEEN → CURRENT → STALE → EXPIRED

Suppose an enemy cavalry observation arrives through an SN.

At observation time, copy the value into AI-side state and record when it was seen. Continue using it while it is fresh. If the observation disappears for too long, let it expire.

This is not merely a coding trick. It is a model of imperfect information.

The engineer must then answer:

    How long is fresh?
    Does every threat class use the same lifetime?
    What happens when a new observation arrives?
    What survives expiry?
    Who owns the expiry rule?

A standing minimum, such as a baseline defensive production floor, may deliberately survive temporary threat expiry. That is different from a temporary threat demand.

This introduces an important distinction:

    volatile observation
    persistent policy

Do not store both as though they had the same lifetime.


13. HYSTERESIS, COOLDOWNS, AND THE ART OF NOT CHANGING YOUR MIND TOO QUICKLY

A bot that changes its mind every time a value crosses a boundary can look irrational even when every individual rule is correct.

Suppose an attack rule activates when superiority is at least one.
Suppose the retreat rule activates when superiority is below one.

At the boundary, tiny fluctuations can cause:

    attack
    retreat
    attack
    retreat

The solution is hysteresis: use different thresholds for entering and leaving a state.

For example:

    enter emergency when condition ≥ 2
    leave emergency only when condition = 0

The gap creates stability.

Cooldowns solve a related problem in time rather than value space. After a retreat, do not immediately re-enter the same battle while units are still repositioning. Arm a timer and wait for the transient to settle.

These patterns are forms of control engineering.

They prevent noise from becoming behavior.

A mature AI therefore does not merely ask:

    "Is the condition true?"

It asks:

    "Has it been true long enough?"
    "Did it just become true?"
    "Did it recently stop being true?"
    "Am I allowed to re-enter yet?"

Once the reader sees this, many apparently mysterious attack and retreat systems become much easier to understand.


14. LOOPS, SEARCH, AND THE DANGEROUS BEAUTY OF NEGATIVE JUMPS

Native .per does not hand you ordinary modern-language loops with a comfortable loop keyword.

Yet mature AIs search.

They scan players, units, states, targets, and other bounded domains.

A common shape is:

    index stored in goal
    ↓
    inspect current candidate
    ↓
    advance index
    ↓
    negative jump back to scan

This is powerful because the same rule region can process several candidates during one pass.

It is dangerous because a negative jump is an actual loop.

If the index never advances, the pass may never finish.

Therefore every .per loop should make a progress argument visible:

    What changes on each iteration?
    What is the maximum value?
    What happens at the bound?
    What happens when there are zero candidates?

A loop without a monotonic progress measure is not merely suspicious. It is a candidate for a real execution freeze.

Naga's player/ally scanning and Shadow's search-state structures demonstrate that sophisticated control flow is not an accidental side effect of .per. It is part of the native engineering vocabulary.


15. GUARD-SKIPS AND COMPUTED DISPATCH

The simplest performance optimization in .per is often to avoid doing work that cannot matter.

A guard can detect that a relevant group is empty and jump over a large targeting block.

Conceptually:

    if no targets exist:
        skip targeting machinery
    otherwise:
        evaluate targeting machinery

The jump distance is positional, so the engineer must treat the block as a frozen region.

A more advanced pattern is computed dispatch.

Naga contains a striking example around its affinity machinery: a caller establishes candidate state, jumps into a rule table, lets one of many behaviors determine an answer, and returns using positional information captured from the rule stream.

This is function-like behavior implemented in a language without conventional subroutines.

The reader should learn to recognize the shape before memorizing its exact implementation:

    establish arguments
    → enter dispatch table
    → execute selected behavior
    → capture result / return position
    → compare or consume result

This is one of the points where .per stops looking like a primitive rules language and starts looking like a tiny machine language for control systems.


16. THE PREPROCESSOR: ONE SOURCE, SEVERAL PROGRAMS

Conditional compilation exists because one AI may need different code for different maps, modes, or environments.

The preprocessor can select blocks before the runtime rule stream exists.

This creates a subtle interaction with jumps:

    preprocessing changes which rules exist
    jumps count rules
    therefore preprocessing can change jump destinations

That implication should become reflexive.

Never say merely "the jump is twelve rules away."

Say:

    "the jump is twelve compiled rules away under this preprocessing configuration."

Every #load-if-defined should have a balanced termination. Nested conditions must be understood as source transformation, not runtime branching.

This is another reason to distinguish source text from compiled rule topology.


17. DEFICIT CORRECTION AND THE QUIET MATHEMATICS OF CONTROL

Resource systems often fail gradually.

A bot does not necessarily collapse because one rule is catastrophically wrong. It can drift because demand grows faster than supply.

A useful pattern is deficit correction:

    desired
    − current
    = deficit

Then:

    if deficit > 0:
        authorize production / gathering / construction

This sounds trivial, but the engineering challenge is choosing what "desired" means and what "current" means.

Current should be an observation.
Desired should belong to policy.
The difference is a requirement for execution.

If the same rule invents desired state, checks resources, spends resources, trains the unit, and verifies the result, it has become difficult to reason about and difficult to replace.

Separating those concerns is the beginning of architecture.

PART III — HOW A BOT THINKS
======================================================================

18. OBSERVATION COMES BEFORE STRATEGY

A bot cannot reason about a world it has not represented.

The first question in a mature system is therefore not:

    "What should I build?"

It is:

    "What do I know?"

Observation can include:

    enemy military census
    enemy cavalry count
    enemy ranged count
    own military count
    own resource stock
    building count
    production availability
    age / phase
    local danger
    attack status
    technology status
    timer state
    recent events

An observation should be understood as evidence, not automatically as truth about the entire world.

Line-of-sight information may be incomplete. Engine queries may have specific semantics. Cached observations may be stale.

That is why observation and classification are separate stages.


19. CENSUS: TURNING A WORLD INTO NUMBERS

A census answers questions such as:

    How many cavalry units do I observe?
    How many archers do I have?
    How many military buildings exist?
    How many farms are active?

A census compresses the world.

The compression is useful because policy rules cannot examine every physical detail directly all the time.

But compression creates responsibility.

Every aggregate should have a defined population and lifetime.

"Enemy cavalry count" could mean:

    currently visible cavalry
    last observed cavalry
    all known cavalry
    estimated cavalry

Those are different variables even if they all contain integers.

The engineer therefore writes down the semantic definition before choosing the storage location.


20. CLASSIFICATION: TURNING NUMBERS INTO MEANING

Census produces measurements.

Classification produces categories.

For example:

    cavalry count > 0
        ↓
    cavalry threat

Or:

    local enemy military present + own town under attack
        ↓
    local defense emergency

A classification is more durable than the raw observation because many downstream rules can consume the category.

This is why threat telemetry should not immediately train units.

A clean architecture is:

    observation
        ↓
    classification
        ↓
    requirement
        ↓
    policy
        ↓
    authorization
        ↓
    execution

This chain gives every stage a job.

If a threat module starts spending resources, the boundary has become unclear.


21. CAPABILITY GAPS AND REQUIREMENTS

A threat is not yet a production order.

Enemy cavalry is an observation.
"We lack enough anti-cavalry capability" is a capability-gap statement.

A requirement converts that gap into something the rest of the system can satisfy.

For example:

    observed cavalry threat
        ↓
    anti-cavalry capability insufficient
        ↓
    require additional spearmen
        ↓
    production demand rises

This distinction prevents the AI from confusing enemy description with action.

The same architecture works outside military production:

    too few farms → economic requirement
    insufficient siege → siege requirement
    missing production building → infrastructure requirement
    unsafe forward position → fortification / relocation requirement
    technology dependency unmet → research requirement

The requirement is the bridge between perception and policy.


22. POLICY: WHAT THE BOT WANTS

Policy answers:

    Given the current situation, what should the AI prefer?

Policy should be relatively stable compared with raw observations.

If the enemy cavalry count flickers from 0 to 1 to 0 because of visibility, policy should not necessarily oscillate in exactly the same way.

Policy can establish floors, ceilings, priorities, and desired composition.

A production dial is one possible policy representation.

The important principle is ownership:

    the policy layer decides what is wanted
    the executor decides whether and how it can happen

This is the beginning of demand/executor separation.


23. AUTHORIZATION: WHAT THE BOT IS ACTUALLY ALLOWED TO DO

A desire is not permission.

This distinction becomes essential in complex systems.

Suppose policy says:

    desired spearmen = 6

That does not mean:

    train six spearmen immediately

The bot may lack resources, production infrastructure, population room, a valid production location, or strategic permission to commit resources.

Authorization is the gate between desire and execution.

A useful conceptual chain is:

    policy demand
        ↓
    feasibility
        ↓
    capital availability
        ↓
    arbitration
        ↓
    authorization
        ↓
    executor

Not every AI needs every explicit layer as a separate file. But the engineer should know which conceptual responsibility is being performed somewhere.


24. EXECUTION: LET THE EXISTING MACHINE EXECUTE

One of the most dangerous temptations when extending a mature AI is to rebuild an executor because the policy layer is easier to understand than the existing machinery.

Do not do this casually.

An established executor may contain years of accumulated assumptions about:

    queueing
    placement
    affordability
    producer availability
    interruption
    re-entry
    resource reservation
    tactical conditions
    technology prerequisites
    unit-state constraints

A new policy layer can often improve the decision without replacing the mechanism that physically carries it out.

That is why the engineering pattern:

    policy → demand → existing executor

is so powerful.

It adds intelligence without unnecessarily destroying proven machinery.


25. VERIFICATION: DID THE WORLD CHANGE?

A command is not a result.

This sentence should become instinctive.

The AI can issue a train command.
The engine can accept the command.
The producer can become busy.
The unit can fail to appear because another condition intervened.

Therefore:

    command accepted ≠ world-state completion

Verification closes the loop.

If the desired spearman count was six, the engineer should distinguish:

    demand = 6
    authorization = true
    train command issued
    queue item exists
    unit completed
    census now observes six

Those are separate states.

Verification may use runtime logging, state dumps, replay inspection, or direct observation.

The method should be chosen so that a false positive is difficult.


26. REASSESSMENT: THE BOT'S MOST IMPORTANT HABIT

Every decision becomes stale.

A threat changes.
A unit dies.
A resource is spent.
A building completes.
A technology finishes.
A queue changes.
A timer expires.
A previous authorization becomes invalid.

A mature AI therefore closes its control loop:

    observe → classify → decide → authorize → act → verify → observe again

This is the deeper reason timers, heartbeats, state lifecycles, and verification exist.

They are all ways of managing the fact that the world moves after the rule fires.

A bot that cannot reassess becomes a collection of old decisions.

======================================================================
PART IV — HOW MATURE BOTS ARE ORGANIZED
======================================================================

27. WHY THERE ARE SO MANY FILES

When a beginner sees an AI with dozens of modules, it is tempting to think each file is simply a convenience for keeping the text shorter.

That is only part of the story.

A mature module often corresponds to a conceptual responsibility:

    constants
    initialization
    observations
    threats
    economy
    construction
    production
    military state
    strategy
    diplomacy / interaction
    telemetry
    recovery

The boundary lets the engineer answer:

    Where should I look when this kind of state changes?

But module boundaries are not magical.

The real architecture lives in the dataflow between files.

A tiny module that writes a goal consumed by five other modules may be architecturally more important than a 2,000-line utility library.

Measure architecture by state and authority, not by line count.


28. MODULE BOUNDARIES ARE NOT STATE BOUNDARIES

Two files can both write:

    g:attack-state

Nothing in the filesystem prevents it.

If one module thinks 0 means "idle" and another thinks 0 means "authorized," the system has semantic collision.

Even if both understand the value identically, they may disagree about when they are allowed to change it.

Therefore every important shared state should have:

    semantic definition
    owner
    writers
    readers
    lifecycle
    initialization
    invalidation
    arbitration rules
    verification

This is the state contract.

The engineer should build these contracts before adding large amounts of behavior.


29. INITIALIZATION, MAINTENANCE, POLICY, EXECUTION, TELEMETRY, ARBITRATION, RECOVERY

These labels are not mandatory file names. They are conceptual jobs.

INITIALIZATION establishes defaults.

MAINTENANCE keeps long-lived state coherent.

POLICY decides what should be wanted.

EXECUTION performs feasible engine actions.

TELEMETRY exposes what the system believes is happening.

ARBITRATION resolves competing demands.

RECOVERY restores or abandons state when reality invalidates an assumption.

A single file may perform several jobs in a small AI. In a mature AI they often become more visibly separated.

The engineer's task is to identify the job, not worship the filename.


30. PRODUCER AND CONSUMER

A producer creates a state value or effect.
A consumer uses it.

For example:

    policy rule → writes desired archers
    production rule → reads desired archers

This separation creates a clean contract.

But it also creates a debugging opportunity.

If desired archers rises to ten and no archers appear, the problem must lie somewhere along the chain:

    writer
    → stored value
    → reader
    → feasibility
    → authorization
    → command
    → queue
    → completion
    → verification

This is far easier to debug than one giant rule that tries to perform everything.

Producer/consumer architecture is therefore not just organizational style. It creates traceable causality.


31. DIALS: SEPARATING DESIRE FROM FEASIBILITY

A production dial is one of the most important patterns in mature AI design.

Imagine a threat appears.

Policy reacts immediately and says:

    We want two mangonels.

But the production system may need time to determine whether siege production exists, whether resources are available, whether the current queue is occupied, and whether the unit is still strategically appropriate.

A dial lets policy express desire without pretending that desire is execution.

A common idiom is for policy to raise a ceiling and for producers to consume the dial.

Conceptually:

    POLICY
      ↓
    desired count
      ↓
    PRODUCER
      ↓
    feasibility checks
      ↓
    train
      ↓
    decrement demand / reconcile

Naga provides strong corpus evidence for this style. Its mangonel production behavior demonstrates a policy writer and producer readers around a shared desired-count state, with escrow participation.

Stock demonstrates a different architectural choice in places: some production decisions are made directly in the producer through extensive guards rather than through an explicit demand dial.

Neither should be treated as a universal engine law.

They are different architectural solutions to the same problem.

The engineering question is:

    At what scale does separating desire from execution buy enough clarity to justify the additional state and ownership burden?


32. RESERVATION, ESCROW, AND THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN INTENTION AND MONEY

A bot can want something without being able to pay for it.

It can also decide to reserve resources for something without having physically spent them yet.

These are different states.

A useful accounting vocabulary is:

    policy reserve
    logical commitment
    physical escrow
    authorization
    execution
    completion

Do not collapse them.

The engine provides mechanisms around escrow and research/training execution. Shadow provides especially useful evidence of execution accounting, progression pause, interruption, re-entry, and reconciliation.

For example, an escrow state can control whether an engine action is attempted with escrow semantics. That does not by itself prove that the AI has a separate logical commitment ledger.

The engineer must distinguish:

    what the engine tracks

from:

    what the AI's architecture intends to track.

This distinction is critical when transplanting mechanisms from one bot into another.


33. OWNERSHIP: THE QUESTION THAT SOLVES MORE BUGS THAN ANOTHER RULE

Ask this whenever a system misbehaves:

    Who owns this state?

If nobody can answer, the architecture is already in trouble.

If three modules claim ownership, the architecture is worse.

Ownership means more than "this file writes it most often."

A true owner should define:

    valid values
    transitions
    initialization
    expiration
    conflict rules
    writers
    authority to override
    verification

For a production demand dial, policy may own increases while the executor owns consumption. That is a shared lifecycle with asymmetric authority, not necessarily a conflict.

The important thing is to say so explicitly.


34. COMPETING WRITERS AND SHARED STATE

Suppose module A writes:

    g:attack-state = 1

Module B later writes:

    g:attack-state = 0

Which one wins?

The source order may decide.

That is not arbitration. It is accidental precedence unless deliberately designed.

A proper authority model answers:

    Who can request a change?
    Who can authorize it?
    Who can override another request?
    Under what conditions?
    For how long?
    What happens when the authorization expires?

This becomes particularly important for military state, emergency defense, technology pauses, and production composition.

Never solve competing writers by simply moving one rule lower in the file unless the positional precedence is itself the intended contract and has been documented and tested.


35. AUTHORITY AND ARBITRATION

Multiple subsystems can make individually reasonable requests.

Economy wants villagers.
Military wants spearmen.
Technology wants a research investment.
Construction wants a stable.
Defense wants a tower.

Resources are finite.

The problem is not that one subsystem is wrong.

The problem is that all of them cannot necessarily be satisfied simultaneously.

Arbitration converts competing requirements into an ordered commitment.

A useful conceptual architecture is:

    observations
        ↓
    requirements
        ↓
    competing demands
        ↓
    capital / feasibility analysis
        ↓
    arbitration
        ↓
    commitment
        ↓
    authorization
        ↓
    execution

The exact implementation can be simple or sophisticated. What matters is that the conflict has a home.

If conflict resolution is distributed accidentally across unrelated producer guards, the architecture becomes difficult to reason about.


36. STATE LIFECYCLES: BIRTH, USE, STALENESS, EXPIRY, RE-ENTRY

Every meaningful state should have a lifecycle.

Ask:

    How is it born?
    Who can change it?
    How long is it valid?
    What makes it stale?
    What makes it expire?
    What survives expiry?
    Can it be paused?
    How does it resume?
    What proves it is still valid?

This applies to threat observations, attack authorization, production demands, research intentions, construction requirements, and defensive states.

The lifecycle model prevents a common failure: a value that was correct once becoming permanent simply because nothing ever clears it.

Persistent garbage is one of the most dangerous forms of AI state because it looks stable.


37. FAILURE CONTAINMENT AND RECOVERY

A mature AI assumes that plans can become invalid.

Recovery is therefore not an optional afterthought.

A good recovery system can:

    detect stale authorization
    release obsolete reservations
    clear impossible requirements
    preserve useful policy floors
    pause execution during emergencies
    resume after guards become valid
    reinitialize corrupted local state
    prevent one failed subsystem from poisoning unrelated systems

Shadow's interruption and pause structures are particularly useful study material because they demonstrate that stopping execution does not necessarily mean erasing the strategic state that caused execution to begin.

This is a profound distinction:

    pause ≠ cancel

A pause preserves the possibility of resumption.
A cancel destroys or invalidates the commitment.

An engineer must know which behavior is intended.

PART V — THREE ARCHITECTURES WORTH STUDYING
======================================================================

38. THE STOCK AI: A LARGE HISTORICAL MACHINE

The Stock AI should not be read as a pile of rules.

Read it as accumulated engineering history.

Its size means it contains many generations of solutions, special cases, timing decisions, direct production rules, strategic states, military machinery, and compatibility assumptions.

Its value to an apprentice is precisely that it is not clean.

Clean examples teach syntax.

Large historical systems teach consequences.

When reading Stock, look for:

    initialization blocks
    timers
    strategic-number defaults
    attack state
    retreat logic
    production decisions
    resource guards
    military state transitions
    positional jumps
    recovery behavior

Ask what each region protects.

Do not immediately rewrite a strange pattern because you can imagine a prettier version.

First determine whether the strange code is load-bearing.


39. NAGA: MODULARITY, LIBRARIES, AND REUSABLE CONTROL STRUCTURES

Naga is valuable because its architecture makes conceptual boundaries easier to see.

The root load graph reveals a modular philosophy. Initialization loads supporting libraries. Strategy is separated. Buildings have helpers. Threats have their own machinery. Production uses shared state patterns.

Study Naga at three levels.

First, the module level:

    What responsibility does this file appear to have?

Second, the state level:

    What values does it write and what values do its consumers read?

Third, the control level:

    How does execution move through it?

Naga also demonstrates advanced idioms such as computed dispatch, demand dials, construction producer/consumer chains, threat aggregation, and interaction loops.

Do not copy the syntax blindly.

Extract the mechanism.

Then ask whether your AI has the same problem.


40. SHADOW: DENSITY, SCALE, AND THE POWER OF A MONOLITH

Shadow demonstrates another possibility.

A very large monolithic source can still implement sophisticated control systems.

The absence of a large load graph does not mean absence of architecture. The architecture is encoded in rule neighborhoods, naming, state conventions, control-flow blocks, timers, and repeated interaction patterns.

Shadow is therefore an excellent apprenticeship environment for forensic reading.

If Naga teaches:

    "Architecture can be made visible through modules,"

Shadow teaches:

    "Architecture can be hidden inside a single rule stream and must be reconstructed from behavior and state."

The two lessons are complementary.


41. READING THE SAME PROBLEM IN THREE ARCHITECTURAL DIALECTS

Suppose the problem is "respond to enemy ranged pressure."

In one AI, the answer may be a direct production rule with extensive guards.

In another, it may be:

    threat census
    → classification
    → demand dial
    → producer

In another, it may be buried inside a large military state machine.

The apprentice should learn to translate between these representations.

Do not ask:

    "Which file is the ranged counter file?"

Ask:

    "Where is ranged pressure observed?"
    "Where is it classified?"
    "Where does it become a requirement?"
    "Where is the requirement converted to demand?"
    "Who arbitrates it?"
    "Who executes it?"
    "How is completion observed?"

Those questions survive architectural differences.


42. WRITING STYLE AS ENGINEERING EVIDENCE

Experienced authors leave clues in their code.

Naming reveals conceptual groupings.

Repeated prefixes reveal state families.

Comments reveal intended invariants, although comments can be stale.

Rule adjacency reveals control-flow assumptions.

Long guard chains reveal whether a system is direct or mediated.

Helper files reveal repeated abstractions.

One-shot rules reveal initialization boundaries.

Paired enable/fire or pause/resume rules reveal lifecycle design.

A good reader learns to read code shape before understanding every line.

But style is evidence, not proof.

If twenty rules named "attack-*" exist, that strongly suggests a subsystem. It does not prove that all twenty belong to one authority domain.

Always trace state and consumers.


43. WHY GOOD ENGINEERS SOMETIMES LEAVE UGLY CODE ALONE

Legacy AI code often contains awkward constructs.

A beginner sees duplication.
An experienced engineer asks what the duplication is protecting.

Maybe the code compensates for an engine quirk.
Maybe a jump depends on its exact rule count.
Maybe another module relies on source order.
Maybe a timer is intentionally shared across several rates.
Maybe an apparently redundant guard prevents a transient state.

The rule is not "never refactor."

The rule is:

    understand before simplifying.

A safe refactor preserves observable behavior and architectural invariants, not merely syntax.

PART VI — THE CORPUS AS A TEXTBOOK
======================================================================

44. WHAT THE SYMBOL CORPUS TEACHES

The generated symbol corpus is not merely a dictionary.

Its scale — thousands of symbols spanning commands, strategic numbers, categories, operands, and related metadata — gives the apprentice a map of the language surface.

The purpose of studying it is not to memorize 2,296 entries.

The purpose is to learn how to ask questions about a symbol.

For every command or engine-facing construct, determine:

    exact spelling
    category
    syntax
    arity
    operand types
    store semantics
    fact/action classification
    read/write behavior
    engine interaction
    side effects
    guards
    completion semantics
    failure behavior
    version scope
    observed idioms
    common misuse
    interactions with related commands
    evidence status

The corpus should be mined for clusters.

Which commands manipulate goals?
Which query timers?
Which write engine settings?
Which are dual fact/action constructs?
Which participate in production chains?
Which appear around research escrow?
Which occur in jump idioms?

That is how a reference corpus becomes engineering knowledge.


45. READING A SYMBOL WITHOUT TURNING IT INTO A DICTIONARY ENTRY

Suppose you encounter a command you have never used.

Do not stop at:

    "This command means X."

Ask:

    What problem would require X?
    What state does X touch?
    Does X merely request an engine operation or guarantee an effect?
    What must be true before X?
    What should be observed afterward?
    How do mature AIs use it?
    Is that usage required or conventional?

The final question protects against cargo cult engineering.

A command used in Naga may be useful because Naga solved a problem your bot also has. It may also be a historical artifact specific to Naga's architecture.

Copy the reason, not the appearance.


46. COMMAND CONTRACTS AND FAILURE MODES

Every important command should be treated as a contract.

A contract describes:

    inputs
    reads
    writes
    preconditions
    effects
    completion
    failure
    temporal behavior

For example, an operation that writes a goal should be analyzed separately from an operation that requests a unit train.

The former changes AI state.
The latter asks the engine to alter world state.

Confusing the two is a category error.

Document 1's command-contract material should be used as the authoritative companion reference while working through this book.


47. TIMERS AS CONTROL-SYSTEM COMPONENTS

A timer can implement:

    heartbeat
    cooldown
    delayed re-entry
    periodic census
    retry
    timeout
    staged escalation

That means timer IDs should be treated as architectural resources.

If two modules both assume ownership of the same timer, they may interfere even though neither rule is locally wrong.

Record for every timer:

    owner
    purpose
    enabling rules
    firing rules
    re-arm rules
    cadence
    consumers
    reset behavior

A timer table is often more informative than reading hundreds of individual timer references.


48. GOALS AS SOFTWARE STATE

Goals are deceptively simple.

Because they can hold integers, engineers use them for many purposes:

    counters
    modes
    cursors
    timestamps
    desired counts
    bookmarks
    state machines
    temporary values

This flexibility is both their strength and their danger.

A goal with a vague name becomes a hidden global variable.

A professional naming discipline therefore describes semantic role, not merely storage type.

A state registry should record:

    goal ID
    symbolic name
    semantic type
    writer
    reader
    lifecycle
    valid range
    sentinel values
    reset behavior


49. STRATEGIC NUMBERS AS ENGINE-FACING STATE

Strategic numbers deserve special caution because some are engine-defined and some are used as reusable AI-facing slots.

A slot's number alone is not its meaning.

A mature AI may intentionally repurpose a slot under a defined convention.

The engineer must establish:

    whether the SN is engine-owned
    whether the AI writes it
    whether the AI reads it
    whether another system changes it
    what version assumptions apply

Naga's threat aliases provide concrete examples of symbolic names mapped onto SN IDs for infantry, archers, cavalry, siege, monks, and related categories.

Again: corpus usage establishes a usage pattern. It does not automatically establish universal semantics for every context.


50. JUMPS AS A NATIVE CONTROL-FLOW LANGUAGE

Once the reader becomes comfortable with jumps, an important realization occurs:

The .per language has a control-flow vocabulary richer than its surface syntax suggests.

There are:

    forward guard-skips
    negative search loops
    computed dispatch
    direct jumps
    bookmarks
    positional returns

These mechanisms form a low-level control language.

The engineer should document jump regions like code documents function boundaries in another language.

For every nontrivial jump region record:

    source rule
    target rule
    delta
    purpose
    guard
    assumptions about intervening rules
    preprocessor constraints
    edit sensitivity

A jump without a documented invariant is technical debt with a numeric disguise.


51. COMMON IDIOMS AND WHAT THEY REVEAL

The corpus repeatedly demonstrates patterns such as:

    true → initialization → disable-self
    timer-triggered → work → re-arm
    observation → latch → timestamp
    low threshold → enter
    high threshold → exit
    index → inspect → increment → negative jump
    cheap absence test → forward skip
    candidate setup → dispatch table → return
    policy demand → producer feasibility → execution
    escrow spend → demand reconciliation
    pause → preserve authorization → resume after recheck
    census → classification → requirement → policy

These are the alphabet of experienced .per engineering.

Once they become familiar, reading a large AI becomes less like translating every sentence and more like recognizing words.

That is fluency.

Engineering begins when you know when not to use the word.


52. NEGATIVE KNOWLEDGE: LEARNING FROM WHAT DOES NOT WORK

An expert guide must teach failure, not just successful patterns.

Important failure classes include:

    wrong store prefix
    constant substituted for state
    duplicate writer
    missing reader
    missing re-arm
    stale latch
    expired state treated as current
    direct execution bypassing policy
    demand with no executor
    executor with no demand contract
    escrow without reconciliation
    source-order dependency hidden in a jump
    jump across conditional compilation
    unbounded negative loop
    initialization occurring after maintenance
    cancellation mistaken for pause
    command accepted mistaken for completion
    world completion assumed without verification

A system becomes easier to engineer when failure modes have names.

======================================================================
PART VII — BECOMING AN ENGINEER
======================================================================

53. THE ELEVEN FAILURES OF LOCAL REASONING

Local reasoning means looking at a rule and deciding whether it is correct without tracing its surroundings.

It fails repeatedly in mature AIs.

Failure 1 — The correct command reads the wrong store.

Failure 2 — The producer is correct but its demand writer never fires.

Failure 3 — The demand is correct but another writer lowers it later.

Failure 4 — The producer issues a command but the world never reaches completion.

Failure 5 — The world completes but verification state is never updated.

Failure 6 — A timer fires correctly once but is never re-armed.

Failure 7 — A jump was correct before a harmless-looking insertion.

Failure 8 — A threat classifier is correct but its state expires too quickly.

Failure 9 — A paused authorization is accidentally canceled.

Failure 10 — A resource reservation is logically present but physical escrow is not.

Failure 11 — Two modules use the same state name with different meanings.

The common cause is the same:

    the bug is not inside the line being inspected.

The solution is to widen the trace.


54. ENGINEERING MYSTERIES

A good apprentice should learn to enjoy the strange behavior of a bot.

Consider these cases.

MYSTERY A — "It worked once."

A strategy activates correctly at the beginning and then never responds again.

First suspect: heartbeat without re-arm.

MYSTERY B — "The number is right, but the bot behaves as if it is zero."

Trace the store prefix.

MYSTERY C — "The bot keeps wanting more units forever."

Trace the producer's consumption and reconciliation of demand.

MYSTERY D — "The attack toggles every few seconds."

Check hysteresis, cooldowns, and competing authority before rewriting the attack logic.

MYSTERY E — "Adding one innocent rule broke an unrelated system."

Look for positional jump dependence.

MYSTERY F — "The command appears in the log, but the expected unit never arrives."

Separate command acceptance from world-state completion.

MYSTERY G — "The bot suddenly spends all its resources on one priority."

Trace competing demands, capital arbitration, escrow, and priority ownership.

MYSTERY H — "The bot remembers an enemy that has not been seen for ages."

Trace latch timestamps and expiry.

These mysteries are not tricks. They are training for the exact moment an AI behaves differently from the engineer's mental model.


55. HOW TO READ AN UNFAMILIAR AI WITHOUT GETTING LOST

Never begin with the largest file.

Begin at the root.

Phase 1 — Identify the entry point.

Phase 2 — Record the load graph.

Phase 3 — Identify constants and initialization.

Phase 4 — Build a state inventory.

Phase 5 — Find major writer families.

Phase 6 — Find consumers.

Phase 7 — Reconstruct control loops and timers.

Phase 8 — Trace one important behavior end to end.

Phase 9 — Find competing writers and authority conflicts.

Phase 10 — Verify the reconstructed model against runtime or replay evidence.

The first behavior to trace should be narrow enough to finish.

For example:

    enemy cavalry
        → cavalry classification
        → counter requirement
        → desired spearmen
        → production reader
        → train command
        → completed spearman

One complete trace teaches more than reading 5,000 lines without a model.


56. HOW TO DESIGN A BOT FROM A BLANK FOLDER

A blank project is not a blank page.

It is an opportunity to define contracts before accidents become architecture.

Start with requirements.

Then define domains.

Then define state.

Then define ownership.

Then define observation.

Then define policy.

Then define execution.

Then define verification.

Then define recovery.

Then choose modules.

Then define load order.

Then implement the smallest closed loop.

The first useful loop should be end-to-end:

    observation → decision → command → world effect → verification

A system that has ten policy rules but no verified world effect is less complete than a system with one fully closed loop.


57. HOW TO ADD A SUBSYSTEM TO A LIVING BOT

Do not start by writing rules.

Start by finding the existing mechanism.

Ask:

    Does the bot already observe this?
    Does it already classify it?
    Does it already have a production executor?
    Does it already have a resource allocator?
    Does it already have a timer?
    Does it already have recovery?

Reuse proven mechanisms wherever possible.

Then define the new boundary.

A strong extension often looks like:

    existing observation
        ↓
    new classification / policy
        ↓
    existing demand state or a new declared state
        ↓
    existing executor
        ↓
    existing verification

This is safer than duplicating the entire pipeline.


58. HOW TO REFACTOR WITHOUT DESTROYING LOAD-BEARING BEHAVIOR

Before changing mature code, build a baseline.

Record:

    parser validity
    load graph
    rule counts
    constants
    goals
    SNs
    timers
    jump regions
    writer/reader relationships
    representative runtime behavior
    replay evidence

Then change one architectural boundary at a time.

After each change, verify:

    syntax
    structural integrity
    state ownership
    control flow
    runtime behavior
    world-state result

Never use a smaller diff as a substitute for a safer diff.

A ten-line change can be more dangerous than a thousand-line addition if those ten lines alter a jump target or shared state owner.


59. HOW TO PROVE A CHANGE WORKED

Verification should be designed before implementation.

For every change write a hypothesis:

    If condition X occurs, state Y should change from A to B.

Then define the observation:

    log
    state dump
    runtime marker
    replay count
    world-state event

Then define the failure interpretation.

Example:

    Hypothesis:
    enemy cavalry classification causes anti-cavalry demand to rise.

    Expected trace:
    cavalry observation > 0
    → classification active
    → demand goal increases
    → producer sees demand
    → train command issued
    → completed counter unit observed

If the trace stops between classification and demand, the policy bridge failed.

If demand rises but production does not act, the executor path failed.

If production acts but completion never occurs, the engine/world path failed.

This is causal debugging.


60. FROM SYNTAX TO SYSTEMS: THE THREE BOT APPRENTICESHIP

A useful apprenticeship model has three stages.

BOT ONE — FLUENCY

The apprentice learns the language.

They can write rules, use conditions and actions, manipulate goals, understand SNs, use timers, load modules, and avoid common parser mistakes.

The test is:

    Can you write valid .per without constantly consulting a syntax reference?

BOT TWO — SYSTEMS THINKING

The apprentice learns to build interacting subsystems.

They can separate observation, policy, execution, and verification. They can define state ownership and trace data across files.

The test is:

    Can you explain why the bot behaves as a system rather than as a collection of rules?

BOT THREE — ENGINEERING

The apprentice can enter an unfamiliar AI, reconstruct it, change it, and prove the change.

They can recognize idioms, infer hidden contracts, distinguish evidence from assumption, preserve load-bearing behavior, and design new mechanisms from established patterns.

The test is:

    Can you make a significant change without needing someone else to tell you where the architecture is?

That is the graduate standard of this book.


61. INTUITION DRILLS

The following questions should become automatic.

SEE THIS:

    (disable-self)

ASK THIS:

    Is this initialization? What must happen before maintenance?

SEE THIS:

    (timer-triggered 7)

ASK THIS:

    Who enabled timer 7? Who re-arms it?

SEE THIS:

    (up-jump-rule 44)

ASK THIS:

    What exactly is being skipped? What happens if I insert a rule?

SEE THIS:

    (up-jump-rule -2)

ASK THIS:

    Where is the loop's progress measure and bound?

SEE THIS:

    (up-modify-goal X c:max Y)

ASK THIS:

    Who owns X? Is this policy demand? Who consumes it?

SEE THIS:

    (count g:X)

ASK THIS:

    Who writes X? When does it expire? Is it really the intended goal?

SEE THIS:

    (up-train ...)

ASK THIS:

    What proves that training actually completed?

SEE THIS:

    two files writing the same goal

ASK THIS:

    Where is authority defined?

SEE THIS:

    a comment saying "temporary"

ASK THIS:

    What actually clears it?

SEE THIS:

    a module called recovery

ASK THIS:

    What state can become invalid, and what evidence triggers recovery?

These questions turn code reading into engineering observation.


62. GRADUATION EXAMINATION

The final exercise should not be a syntax quiz.

Give the apprentice an unfamiliar AI.

Do not tell them its architecture.
Do not tell them which file contains the important mechanism.
Do not tell them which behavior is broken.

Require the apprentice to produce:

1. root/load graph
2. module responsibility map
3. state inventory
4. writer/reader matrix
5. timer inventory
6. jump/control-flow map
7. evidence classification
8. one complete observation-to-world trace
9. identification of competing writers
10. proposed ownership boundaries
11. a new requirement
12. architecture for satisfying it
13. implementation
14. verification plan
15. failure injection
16. recovery behavior
17. runtime or replay evidence
18. explanation of what remains uncertain

The final system should be tested under interference.

Do not test only:

    threat → counter unit

Test:

    threat + low resources
    threat + technology demand
    threat + construction demand
    threat + existing production queue
    threat + emergency defense
    threat disappears before production completes
    authorization expires during execution
    competing demand appears
    executor fails to complete
    stale observation remains

This is where engineering competence becomes visible.


63. THE PROFESSIONAL STANDARD

The finished engineer no longer reads .per from left to right and hopes understanding emerges.

They read it through several lenses.

LANGUAGE LENS

What does the engine literally accept?

STATE LENS

Where does information live, who owns it, and how long is it valid?

CONTROL LENS

How does execution move through the rule stream?

DATAFLOW LENS

Where does an observation go next?

AUTHORITY LENS

Who is allowed to make the decision?

RESOURCE LENS

What is desired, committed, reserved, spent, and verified?

TEMPORAL LENS

What happens now, later, periodically, after expiry, or after interruption?

FAILURE LENS

What happens when an assumption becomes false?

EVIDENCE LENS

What do we actually know, and what are we merely inferring?

ARCHITECTURE LENS

Why is the system divided this way?

The mature engineer can move among these lenses without losing the concrete code.

That is the purpose of this book.

APPENDIX A — DESIGN VOCABULARY
======================================================================

ACTION — A construct that changes AI or engine state.

AUTHORIZATION — A state indicating that a requested action is permitted to proceed.

CAPABILITY GAP — A difference between required capability and currently available capability.

CENSUS — Aggregation of observations into counts or measurements.

CLASSIFICATION — Transformation of observations into meaningful categories or states.

COMMITMENT — A logical decision that the system intends to honor, distinct from physical execution.

CONSUMER — A rule family that reads state produced by another component.

CONTROL LOOP — Observation followed by decision, action, verification, and reassessment.

COOLDOWN — A temporal guard preventing immediate re-entry after a transition.

DEMAND DIAL — AI state expressing desired production or capacity, consumed by an executor.

DIRECT — Evidence observed directly in a corpus or runtime artifact.

ESCROW — Resource accounting associated with an engine action or execution path.

EXPIRY — Transition that makes previously valid state no longer authoritative.

FACT — A predicate or engine observation used as rule evidence.

HEARTBEAT — A periodically re-triggered control cycle.

HYSTERESIS — Different thresholds for entering and leaving a state.

INFERRED — Reasoned interpretation not directly demonstrated.

INITIALIZATION — One-time establishment of starting state.

LATCH — Persistent copy of volatile information retained beyond the immediate observation.

MAINTENANCE — Ongoing rules that preserve or update established state.

MODULE — A source-level organizational unit with a conceptual responsibility; not automatically an isolated state domain.

OBSERVATION — Evidence about the current or recently observed world.

ONE-SHOT — A rule or operation intended to occur once.

OWNER — Component responsible for the semantic authority and lifecycle of a state value.

POLICY — Rules deciding what should be wanted or preferred.

PRODUCER — A component that creates a state value or physical effect.

REASSESSMENT — Re-evaluation after state or world conditions change.

RECOVERY — Mechanism for restoring coherence after invalidation or interruption.

RE-ENTRY — Returning to an activity after interruption, with guards rechecked.

REQUIREMENT — An actionable statement of needed capability or state.

RESERVATION — Intentional allocation of resources or capacity toward a future action.

STRATEGIC NUMBER — Engine-facing numeric state or reusable AI strategic-number slot.

TEMPORAL GUARD — A condition involving elapsed time, timer state, freshness, or cooldown.

TELEMETRY — Instrumentation exposing internal state for diagnosis or verification.

VERIFICATION — Evidence that the intended state or world effect actually occurred.

APPENDIX D — RECOMMENDED PRACTICE PROJECTS
======================================================================

PROJECT 1 — FIRST RULE

Create a minimal AI that initializes one state value, changes it under a condition, and logs the transition.

Goal: learn the rule/pass/state model.

PROJECT 2 — TIMER HEARTBEAT

Build a periodic counter with explicit enable, trigger, and re-arm logic.

Goal: understand temporal control.

PROJECT 3 — LATCHED OBSERVATION

Take a volatile observation, copy it to persistent AI state, timestamp it, and expire it.

Goal: understand state lifetime.

PROJECT 4 — BOUNDED SEARCH

Scan a finite player or index domain with a goal cursor and negative jump.

Goal: understand native looping and prove termination.

PROJECT 5 — DEMAND/EXECUTOR

Create a policy dial and an executor that consumes it only when feasibility conditions hold.

Goal: separate desire from execution.

PROJECT 6 — AUTHORIZATION

Add a competing demand and an arbitration rule that decides which demand receives permission.

Goal: understand authority.

PROJECT 7 — VERIFIED PRODUCTION

Connect policy demand to a real production action and verify completion in runtime or replay evidence.

Goal: close the control loop.

PROJECT 8 — RECOVERY

Interrupt the activity after authorization but before completion. Resume only after rechecking guards.

Goal: distinguish pause, cancel, and re-entry.

PROJECT 9 — FORENSIC RECONSTRUCTION

Take an unfamiliar AI and reconstruct its root, modules, state, timers, jumps, and one complete behavior without changing the source.

Goal: learn to read before editing.

PROJECT 10 — ENGINEERING CHANGE

Add a new capability requirement while preserving existing production, resource, and military behavior. Produce a written state/authority contract and verification evidence.

Goal: demonstrate independent engineering.

FINAL WORD
======================================================================

The novice eventually learns what a rule is.

Then they learn what a timer is, what a goal is, what an SN is, what a jump is, what a producer is, what a dial is, what a module is.

Those are necessary discoveries, but they are not the destination.

The destination is a different way of looking.

A line of .per stops being merely a line of .per.

It becomes a state transition.

A state transition becomes part of a lifecycle.

The lifecycle becomes part of a subsystem.

The subsystem becomes one participant in an authority structure.

The authority structure becomes part of a control loop.

The control loop becomes part of the bot's architecture.

And the architecture becomes something the engineer can inspect, question, change, and prove.

That is the progression this apprenticeship is designed to produce:

    from syntax
    to idiom
    to pattern
    to mechanism
    to state
    to subsystem
    to architecture
    to engineering judgment.

The final skill is not knowing every command.

It is knowing what question to ask when you encounter a command you have never seen before.

It is knowing where a decision came from.

It is knowing who owns it.

It is knowing what happens when reality disagrees with it.

And, most importantly, it is knowing how to prove what the machine actually did.

That is when the reader has stopped merely writing .per.

That is when the reader has become a .per engineer.
